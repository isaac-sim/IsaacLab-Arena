# G1 loco-manipulation task plan

The [30-task G1 catalog](LOCO_CATALOG.md) lists the three controls and 27 selected challenges, with a real-life example and the scored operation for each.

Tasks 1–3 are the easier spatial-memory controls and count toward the 30 tasks. Tasks 4–30 must also be evaluated against a controller that remembers a compact route. Each uses the head camera, a physically grasped box, and the existing G1 walking and squeeze postures. A shelf reach means walking up to a shallow opening at the demonstrated hand height. Reach depth, clearance and stable release need measurement.

## Learned depth-cloud acceptance

The experimental input is the episode's registered XYZ surface samples, updated online from the onboard depth camera. Use the same current RGB-D, proprioception, instruction, localization, actions, demonstrations, training budget and seed splits for all policies. Compare the accumulated cloud with a current-frame cloud, a three-second history, a recurrent RGB-D policy and a compact route controller that can recombine stored segments. Compare matched scenes using intact, erased and swapped hidden geometry at the decision. Give every cloud the same capacity, coordinate frame and validity convention. Keep semantic labels, layout parameters and task state out of the policy inputs.

Train on independent layouts. For each new task, report full delivery with confidence intervals across held-out layouts and variation across training seeds. Use paired tests for the cloud advantage; separately report the first irreversible action, collisions, dropped loads and timeouts. For the 27 challenge tasks, use the existing 45/50 delivery target for the learned cloud policy and the 15/50 ceiling for the current-view and short-history baselines. Require the paired advantage over the strongest tested recurrent or compact-route policy to exclude zero. Report every training seed. The example `compare_delivery_ablations.py` checks 50 paired episode records per method for identical task, seed, episode and layout, then reports wins, losses and a paired interval. Use a separate directory for each task and training seed. The privileged oracle crossing the original threshold establishes feasibility only. A task with a shortcut, a failed learned cloud policy, or insufficient evidence against the strongest competing policy remains experimental. The three pilots remain controls. An unrestricted recurrent policy can in principle reconstruct the same geometry from its sensor history.

Screen each proposed mechanism before adding it to the challenge count. A delayed query must refer to a fact recoverable from measured surfaces. For shelves, use the measured interior, protrusions and insertion envelope. For exits, use mapped free passages, their connections and clearance for the load. A plaque-colour lookup or replaying the inbound walk is insufficient evidence. Vary the pickup pose, load approach or receiving request after a common survey so that different routes can be needed through the same static geometry. Confirm that the discriminating surfaces actually survive projection, voxel sampling and the point budget in the cloud delivered to the policy.

For a claim about what is possible with current observations alone, construct matched layouts with identical accessible sensor histories over the allowed window and disjoint successful first choices. With four equiprobable alternatives, the best common choice succeeds on at most one quarter. Rendered similarity or a failed training run alone does not establish that bound.

A useful design check is how much a controller must retain at the end of inspection. For example, consider 16 independently randomized shelf recesses, each with one of four possible clear insertion directions. Reveal a uniformly chosen recess only at pickup. Perfect decisions require distinguishing all four choices at each recess: 4¹⁶ layouts, or at least 32 bits of persistent state. With no retained information, the single-guess ceiling is 25%. This bound assumes that later observations, including robot pose and manipulation state, carry no layout information before commitment. It also explains why even this larger design could be solved by a short table of directions. The claim about the usefulness of a point cloud must come from performance on new shapes, viewpoints and clearance queries.

The original partition control used four equal-size, equal-height plaques. Permuting their colors leaves XYZ unchanged while changing the requested room. Raising the four identical markers to different mounting heights made the room identity visible in depth, but 15 of the first 50 physical trials had already ended without success because the survey missed one marker. They cannot meet the 45/50 threshold. The separately registered experimental environment `spatial_memory_partition_silhouettes` keeps all four marker centers and horizontal footprints in their original positions, with four different plaque heights. The original room control retains its identical plaques. For the new silhouettes, a nominal analytic sweep revealed all four plaques on 128/128 layouts and none at the common pickup view, at each of four tested mount heights. In a standard episode-limited simulation run on 50 layouts, the controller with layout access delivered 45 cartons and the fixed-choice controller delivered 11. The visibility recorder counted all four markers on inspection in every trial and zero marker sightings during the hidden phase. Two robots collided. A separate earlier diagnostic with automatic replacements and a batch-wide walking-controller reset returned zero deliveries. The episode-limited result supersedes that diagnostic.

In a single-environment intervention on the reverse-gap control, the same robot state faced the pickup table while the four barrier openings were substituted behind it. Against the reference opening, 0/307,200 clipped depth pixels differed by more than 1 cm for any alternative. From the survey pose the three alternatives changed 67,876, 67,867 and 2,389 pixels at that tolerance. RGB varied across interventions and across repeat renders of an identical layout. This checks a single prescribed pickup view and does not certify indistinguishability for arbitrary RGB-D histories or searching policies.

A supervised lane classifier trained on 128 prescribed depth surveys per control received voxel histograms in the robot frame and the requested marker where applicable. It was evaluated separately on 64 held-out layouts per task.

They do not measure walking or release. In a separate run on the shelf, 64 training surveys gave 23/32 correct choices on prescribed held-out poses. On one independent shelf layout, rendered stock-instance masks showed why acquisition matters. Only one stock unit appeared in the two endpoint views. An eleven-view sweep with fixed posture exposed all three, with peak mask sizes of 4,906, 10,462 and 4,327 pixels. At pickup the registered cloud retained respectively 3, 65 and 5 voxel centers inside the three stock bounding boxes expanded by 3.5 cm on each face. Segmentation and ground-truth boxes were used only to audit the images and points. Sparse coverage can remain a problem even after every object has been seen. When the classifier was trained and evaluated on a stabilized eleven-view sweep, it chose the correct shelf in 32/32 of the same held-out layouts (64 training layouts), versus 10/32 with current depth and 1/32 with swapped clouds. Both survey density and posture stabilization changed in this comparison. In the same two physical layouts as the endpoint trial, these new weights chose the second and leftmost bays. Both choices were wrong before either robot finished an episode, and neither delivery succeeded.

Training on actual walks addressed this mismatch in a small independent trial. Eight layouts provided cloud histograms at the first public instruction. On four held-out layouts in the same simulation batch, accumulated depth chose 4/4 correct bays, current depth chose 1/4, and permuting the four clouds gave 2/4. The G1 completed all four cloud-guided deliveries with scripted gait, grasp and release. Three held-out targets were leftmost and one rightmost. No center bay was requested. These results cover one seed and four deliveries. Each environment had a single assigned episode. The fresh-batch probe supports collecting these features with `--survey walking`.

A second training seed was chosen using the layout generator before fitting: two training walks per bay and one held-out walk per bay. The policy selected 2/4 correct bays with accumulated depth, 1/4 with current depth and 1/4 with swapped clouds. The independent walking trial completed 2/4 deliveries. Across the two small studies, the learned cloud decisions yielded 6/8 full deliveries; current and swapped controls were compared on bay choice only. The recordings include all four held-out walks from the balanced split.

## Trial design

For each row, generate four equally likely answers by permuting the relevant geometry independently of starting pose. Jitter start position, approach distance and fixture positions within physically reachable bounds. Use the same distribution for both controllers. Where the answer is a continuous location, sample four separated feasible regions with disjoint success tolerances. The blind strategy follows the same inspection and handling motions and commits to a fixed feasible answer. Its expected success is at most one in four **if** the current view, instruction and proprioception contain no answer. Compare matched episodes with different correct answers at the same decision pose, including recent frames, to check that assumption.

For tasks 4–30, give the compact-route and map-based controllers the same inbound observations, pose estimates, action interface and inspection time. Specify what the compact controller retains for each task, including its coordinate precision and waypoint budget. Tune it on separate layouts. On held-out paired layouts, report the first route decision, carried-object clearance, collisions and final delivery separately. A grasp or release failure cannot establish a spatial reasoning advantage. Compare the reconstructed map with its hidden portion erased or exchanged between layouts. Record the layout oracle separately as the feasibility ceiling.

The inspection must expose enough geometry to distinguish all four answers. Track the actual discriminating surfaces as remembered targets. Return to a common decision pose after inspection to prevent the final inspection pose from encoding the answer. Reveal the query only after the targets have been hidden longer than the three-second history. Latch the first physical commitment (a crossed boundary, grasp or placement, as specified below). Fail a wrong first commitment even if later corrected. Count rediscovery before commitment as failure. Require physical manipulation and the correct final object pose for success.

## Compact-route checks on the first seven prototypes

For each prototype, compare its oracle plan with the same waypoint generator given only route coordinates rounded to centimetres. Across 256 sampled layouts per prototype, all seven preserved the waypoint sequence, arm commands and headings, with a maximum position difference below 5.1 mm. The dogleg, turning pocket, two-gate exit, funnel and post turn each used one coordinate. The staggered shelf lips used two and the curved channel used five. This check starts from ideal perception. It measures how much information the planner needs after inspection. Physical execution and extracting the coordinates from images need separate tests.

A separate two-gate check used only the rendered head-camera images at two fixed survey poses. A template classifier trained on 32 layouts retained one bit from each gate and selected the correct lane on all 64 held-out layouts (all four lanes represented). This was a route-selection test. Walking, grasping and delivery were scored separately. The two-gate prototype therefore fails as evidence that an explicit reconstruction is needed.

On the three controls, a separate privileged diagnostic retained the lane index and discarded the continuous offset. It crossed the correct entrance in 49/50 reverse-gap trials, 48/50 shelf trials and 45/50 partition trials. Final deliveries were respectively 0/50, 26/50 and 0/50. The low delivery rates include handling and alignment failures after the correct choice. These diagnostic runs allowed completed environments to reset while others were walking. The Homie reset implementation at the time cleared the whole batch’s gait and observation history. Their final delivery rates therefore confound alignment and gait disruption. The standard episode-limited oracle and fixed-choice evaluations used one episode per environment.

## Column-grid replanning probe

The first column grid disclosed only the dock at pickup. Across 96 held-out depth surveys and four dock requests, two stored routes and replanning on the map each produced 326 valid choices out of 384. The revised task discloses two inspection points along with the dock. The same unchanging grid consequently supports 64 requests after pickup.

On 96 fresh layouts, the probe rendered depth from the G1 head at six prescribed survey poses with small independent pose jitter. It used calibrated camera localization, the known row and lane coordinates, and depth returns accumulated in those cells to estimate which passages were free. It identified both openings in 256 of 288 rows when tied depth-return counts were resolved in ascending lane order. Some occluded cells returned no points. Changing the ordering of tied cells reduced exact row recovery to 174/288 on the identical depth measurements; the score depends on that sensor-side prior. Every controller received the same estimated openings during inspection. Before seeing the request, the route controllers selected a library that maximized coverage of all possible requests on the estimated grid. After reveal, each chose its shortest stored route for the request. The map controller retained the openings and planned after reveal.

| Memory retained from the survey | Valid route decisions out of 6,144 |
|---|---:|
| One route | 2,290 (37.3%) |
| Two routes | 3,745 (61.0%) |
| Four routes | 4,695 (76.4%) |
| Free passages by row | 4,847 (78.9%) |
| Two routes with switching at gantries | 4,847 (78.9%) |
| Free passages from another layout | 440 (7.2%) |

A valid decision selected a free passage in every row and met the route-length budget for the requested inspection points and dock. Across layouts, the paired advantage over four stored routes was 2.47 percentage points (95% layout-bootstrap interval 1.87–3.08). These measurements cover route selection at nominal survey poses. Acceptance still requires actual head-camera surveys during walking, successful pickup and delivery, and the suite checks.

Allowing a route controller to switch between two complementary routes at the gantries recovers every available combination of openings. A fresh simulation run and a replay of the 96 saved surveys agreed on the totals in the table. The map and the route-switching controller produced the same number of valid choices on every layout. The row-wise grid therefore demonstrates an advantage over replaying a fixed route library, while a compact, recombinable route representation suffices. Keep this design experimental.

A separate learned probe used the generic 4,096-point observation with no task-specific accumulation during the survey. Training used 80 layouts and predicted free lanes from voxel histograms. On 48 additional layouts and 64 requests per layout, it made 1,464/3,072 valid route decisions (47.7%). Current-frame depth made 116 (3.8%), and swapped clouds made 272 (8.9%). Two recombinable routes computed from the same predicted openings again matched the map on every request in aggregate. This is a geometry and route-selection probe at prescribed poses. In a separate matched walking trial using task-specific depth bins, the replanning controller and the two- and four-route controllers each reached the requested dock, released the carton upright and met the distance budget. They selected the same crossings. The one-route controller also released the carton at the requested dock, but exceeded the distance budget and timed out. This was one layout with a shared query, using a different depth representation from the 4,096-point probe. The trial ran before the batch reset fix, so later finishing times may include gait disruptions.

## Switchback geometry screen

A geometric prototype used four parallel aisles, three dividers and five independently positioned crossovers per divider. Each of eight transfer pads could be paired with each of eight receiving stations. The compact controller retained up to four crossover locations per divider before the request and could switch between remembered routes wherever the layout allowed. With exact geometry and a 0.35 m distance allowance it found valid paths for 6,090/6,144 requests across 96 layouts. The complete geometry found 6,144. This layout also falls short of the intended difficulty. It has no simulated delivery result.

## Evidence required before accepting a new task

1. **Build matched counterexamples.** For each task, construct four layouts with the same inbound actions, pickup pose, query, carton, visible local geometry and recent head-camera history at the decision. Change only the previously observed exit geometry. Include start and fixture jitter independently of the answer. Record the correct commitment and show that the sets of collision-free successful commitments do not overlap. For two-stage binary tasks, check this at each stage. The 25% chance bound depends on equally likely alternatives and indistinguishable observations.
2. **Try to defeat the design.** Run the fixed-choice blind controller, a current-view controller that can steer and follow a wall, and a controller that reverses the inbound motion using odometry. Also test the three-second frame history. Allow looking around and inexpensive recovery in a diagnostic run with the search penalty disabled. If a controller can reliably exit by peeking, probing, reversing the path or moving slowly along a wall, revise the geometry or record the task only as a test of avoiding repeated search.
3. **Test what the map contributes.** Give the same controller an occupancy map built from the inbound head-camera observations, then erase or swap the hidden part of that map at pickup while preserving pose, instruction and visible obstacles. Compare completion, first choice and first contact on matched seeds. The privileged layout oracle establishes physical feasibility. A successful map-assisted controller and a drop under map removal provide separate evidence for the value of reconstruction. A policy can also retain sufficient spatial information internally or as a compact route description.
4. **Apply the pilot thresholds.** Require at least 45/50 privileged oracle successes and at most 15/50 successes for each tested memoryless baseline. Keep deterministic reset, all four observation variants and per-episode records. Check earlier visibility, the history-length delay and every claimed hidden phase in solved episodes. Cross-check new occlusion mechanisms against rendered instance masks. Record paired head-camera videos through pickup, commitment and release.
5. **Measure feasibility first.** Use the existing whole-body controller, two-hand squeeze grasp, shallow reachable supports and static fixtures. Measure unloaded and loaded swept volumes before setting clearances. Check scene spacing, walking time and grasp reliability. A lane that the oracle cannot traverse reliably is a failed design even if all blind policies collide.

Start with 4, 5 and 20. They isolate longitudinal clearance, loaded turning space and integration of two partial views. Their failures can be diagnosed separately before combining mechanisms in task 30.
